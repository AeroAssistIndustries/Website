A local business website has one job: help someone who already found you decide to call, book or walk in. It doesn't need to be big or clever. It needs to be fast, clear and easy to use on a phone. Here's what matters, and what you can safely skip.

Start with the job, not the design

Most people who visit a local business website already know roughly what they want. They came from Google Maps, a social post or a friend's recommendation, and they're checking a few things before they act: Are you open? Do you do what I need? What does it cost? Where are you? Can I book now?

A good site answers those questions in seconds and puts the next step, a call, a booking or directions, one tap away on every page. Beautiful photos and a strong brand help, but only after the basics are covered.

The must-haves

1. It works beautifully on a phone

Most local searches happen on phones, often on the go. Google also looks at the mobile version of your site first when deciding how to rank it. Check your own site on your phone: Is the text readable without zooming? Are buttons big enough to tap? Does anything run off the side of the screen?

2. Your phone number, address and hours are easy to find

Put them in the header or footer of every page, written as text rather than inside an image, so phones can tap to call and Google can read them. Make sure they match your Google Business Profile exactly, including suite numbers and holiday hours.

3. A clear next step on every page

Decide on the one action you want most, such as "Book a table", "Get a quote" or "Call now", and make it the most obvious button on the page. On a phone, a button that stays visible as people scroll works well.

4. A page for each main service

One long page that lists everything makes it harder for both customers and Google to understand what you do. A short page for each main service, with what's included, who it's for, rough pricing if you can share it, and a few common questions, helps people decide and gives you a better chance of showing up for specific searches.

5. Real photos of your place, your team and your work

Stock photos have their place, but nothing builds trust like the real thing. A handful of good, recent photos of your space and your work does more than a gallery of generic images.

6. Proof that others trust you

Show a few genuine reviews, with a link to your Google reviews so people can see the full picture. Never write reviews yourself or edit what customers said.

Speed: the quiet deal-breaker

If a page takes too long to load on a phone, many visitors simply leave and tap the next business on the map. Google measures real-world experience with three Core Web Vitals:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how long the main content takes to appear. Aim for 2.5 seconds or less.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP): how quickly the page responds when someone taps. Aim for 200 milliseconds or less.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): how much things jump around while loading. Aim for 0.1 or less.

The most common fixes for small business sites are simple: resize and compress large photos, remove plugins and widgets you don't use, and avoid huge sliders and autoplay videos at the top of the page. You can test your site with my free website speed check, which uses Google's own testing tool.

Make it easy for everyone to use

An accessible website works for people who use screen readers, zoom in, or can't use a mouse, and it's usually easier for everyone else too. A few basics go a long way:

  • Enough contrast between text and background.
  • Short descriptions (alt text) on meaningful images.
  • Real text for menus and prices, not pictures of text or PDF-only menus.
  • Buttons and links that say where they go, like "View the dinner menu" rather than "Click here".
  • Forms with clear labels on every field.

What you can skip

  • Sliders and carousels at the top of the page. Most people never see past the first slide, and they slow the page down.
  • Pop-ups that appear right away. They're annoying on phones and get in the way of what people came for.
  • A blog you won't keep up. A few genuinely useful pages beat a blog whose last post is from three years ago.
  • Pages and pages of text. Short paragraphs, clear headings and lists are easier to read on a small screen.

Make sure you own it

Whoever builds your site, make sure your domain name is registered in your business's name and that you have the logins for your domain, hosting and any booking or ordering tools. It saves a lot of trouble if you ever change providers.

A quick 10-minute check

  1. Open your site on your phone. Can you find your phone number and hours in five seconds?
  2. Tap the phone number. Does it start a call?
  3. Find the main button, such as Book or Get a quote. Does it work, and does the form actually arrive in your inbox?
  4. Run the speed check. Is the mobile score in decent shape?
  5. Compare your name, address, phone and hours with your Google Business Profile. Do they match exactly?
  6. Look at the photos. Are they recent and real?
